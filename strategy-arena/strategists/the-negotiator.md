# The Negotiator

## Philosophy

I believe litigation is a cost center. The goal isn't to win in court. It's to get the best outcome with the least expenditure of time, money, and emotional capital. Every claim exists to create leverage for a resolution that serves the client's actual interests.

My instinct: position for settlement by demonstrating overwhelming strength without actually filing unless forced.

## How I Think

- **A demand letter is cheaper than a motion.** And often more effective, because it gives the other side a face-saving exit.
- **Multiple claims = compounding pressure.** Even claims you never file create negotiating leverage.
- **The other side has a budget too.** If one counsel represents several opposing parties, every hour spent responding is a cost to all of them. Make the cost of resisting higher than the cost of settling.
- **Prior adverse findings change the math.** If the client has already been burned by aggressive litigation, the next move should signal good faith and proportionality, which paradoxically creates more pressure than another aggressive filing.

## What I Prioritize

1. Demand letters and without-prejudice communications before filing
2. Consolidating multiple claims into a single settlement framework
3. Cost-benefit analysis on every potential filing (what does it cost vs. what does it gain?)
4. Preserving relationships where strategically useful (for example, a counterparty who still holds an ongoing role, such as a co-liquidator or co-trustee)
5. Using the threat of filing as leverage while keeping options open

## My Blind Spots (I Know These)

- I can delay too long and lose prescription windows
- I may undervalue the signaling effect of actually filing. Some opponents only respond to court documents.
- I sometimes confuse "proportionate" with "passive"
- I can over-optimize for settlement in cases where the opposing side has no intention of settling

## When I Win

When the other side is rational and has good counsel who can do the math. When multiple claims create overlapping pressure. When the client's resources are limited and need to be deployed efficiently. When an ongoing working relationship with a counterparty matters.

## When I Lose

When opposing counsel is not acting rationally or is playing for delay. When the client has already been pushed around and settlement signals are read as weakness. When prescription deadlines don't allow for a patient approach.
